
Public aerodynamic benchmark datasets for validating a correlation-based OpenRocket solver
Executive summary
The most important result of this search is that RASAero II’s public validation nucleus is identifiable and narrow: the official comparison page points directly to NACA RM A53D02, NASA TR R-100, NASA TN D-4013, NASA TN D-4014, and Vought Astronautics Report AST/E1R-13319 as the key comparison sources. The ARCAS comparison slides further show that RASAero II uses NASA TN D-4013 and D-4014 to compare center of pressure and power-off drag for ARCAS Short and ARCAS Long configurations, with Reynolds-number matching via its Mach-Alt feature. 

For a deterministic, correlation-based OpenRocket solver, the strongest publicly accessible validation corpus is therefore not a single “RASAero dataset,” but a layered benchmark library built from that RASAero nucleus and expanded with public NASA/NACA reports that cover whole-vehicle drag, transonic and supersonic stability, component isolation, blunt-nose effects, powered base/plume effects, and transonic roll/damping behavior. In this pass, the best verified additions are Suttles 1964, NACA RM L52D15a, NACA RM L53D14a, NACA RM L54D27, NASA TN D-4064, NASA TM 74058, and NACA RM L55G06a as a discovery map. 

The verified public corpus is dominated by experimental, free-flight, and wind-tunnel reports rather than machine-readable CFD archives. Most of the high-value sources are public PDFs that require digitization rather than direct raw-data ingestion. Among the conventional rocket reports verified in this pass, dense whole-vehicle coverage below Mach 0.22 remains thin; Suttles begins at Mach 0.22, while many of the classic NASA/NACA validation reports begin at Mach 0.6 or higher. 

RASAero II validation nucleus
RASAero II’s comparison page is unusually useful because it names the exact public sources used in the vendor’s validation narrative. The page links to NACA RM A53D02, NASA TR R-100, a dedicated ARCAS comparison deck, NASA TN D-4013, NASA TN D-4014, and Vought’s Aerobee 150A report. That gives a concrete, auditable starting point for an OpenRocket benchmark suite rather than a vague claim of “matching RASAero.” 

The ARCAS slide deck is especially informative because it documents not only the source reports but also the comparison setup: RASAero II compares subsonic, transonic, and supersonic CP plus power-off CD for ARCAS Short and ARCAS Long, using NASA TN D-4013 and D-4014 and matching the wind-tunnel Reynolds number of 3.0 × 10^6 per foot. It also shows that the comparison plots are specifically at angle of attack 0°, and that the deck treats D-4013/D-4014 as the key ARCAS validation set.

The direct RASAero nucleus is best treated as follows. A53D02 is the long-range zero-lift drag backbone; R-100 is the broad parametric drag library; D-4013/D-4014 are the most important whole-vehicle transonic/supersonic stability and CP benchmarks; and AST/E1R-13319 is better treated as a vehicle/performance and reference-tracing source than as a clean aerodynamic coefficient dataset, because it points onward to the more primary Aerojet wind-tunnel report for Aerobee 150A.

Coverage matrix and prioritization

L52D15a is the clearest available body-vs-fin / planform-interference benchmark.

On prioritization, I would classify the catalog as follows. Tier 1 core should contain A53D02, TR R-100, D-4013, D-4014, Suttles, L52D15a, L53D14a, L54D27, D-4064, and TM 74058 because together they cover drag, CP/stability, component deltas, powered effects, and roll/damping across the broadest range of Mach and geometry variation. Tier 2 should hold 19660004864, D-5378, and AST/E1R-13319, because they are important but either narrower, more indirect, or less completely verified in this pass. Tier 3 should hold L55G06a, the Spearman review, and industry follow-ons like Aerojet Report 1784, because they are best used to expand the corpus rather than as first-line scored datasets. 

Holdout design and data leakage control
Because you explicitly want the datasets to validate the current solver and possibly later support constant-fitting, the benchmark suite should be split in a way that prevents obvious leakage. I recommend treating A53D02, TR R-100, L53D14a, L52D15a, and L54D27 as the initial development and calibration pool. Those sources give broad drag coverage, clean component or geometry-family variation, and controlled transonic jet/base effects without relying too heavily on a single whole-vehicle family. That recommendation is an inference from the structure of those reports rather than a statement made by the reports themselves. 

I would then lock D-4013, D-4014, Suttles 1964, D-4064, and TM 74058 as the first independent validation holdout family. The reason is coverage diversity: ARCAS gives transonic and supersonic CP/CD/stability on a conventional sounding rocket, Suttles gives a whole-vehicle case with β and component-removal deltas, D-4064 stresses high-Mach plume interaction, and TM 74058 checks whether a static correlation stack is being misapplied to roll/pitch damping regimes. That is exactly the kind of cross-family holdout that catches false gains from tuning on only one physics bucket.

Benchmark implementation
The first implementation step should be to treat benchmark ingestion as a data-engineering problem, not a plotting problem. Every source PDF needs a stable local identity, provenance record, digitization log, and normalized coefficient convention before any solver comparison is trusted. Historical reports in this corpus use different reference areas, reference lengths, moment centers, body/stability axes, and sometimes report only plot-derived data. That is especially clear when you compare the ARCAS reports, the free-flight drag reports, and the transonic dynamic projectile report. 

I would implement the benchmark repository with four layers: source, extracted points, normalized benchmark cases, and comparison results. The source layer stores the PDF, citation metadata, and SHA-256. The extracted points layer stores digitized coordinates with figure/page provenance and a digitization-quality flag. The normalized benchmark cases layer converts each source into OpenRocket’s canonical units and coefficient references. The comparison results layer stores solver predictions, selected correlation methods, validity-domain flags, and error metrics. That structure is justified by how heterogeneous these reports are: some are almost pure drag compilations, some are whole-vehicle force/moment families, some are powered-plume studies, and some are dynamic-derivative reports.

The scored tests should then be separated into a small number of clear families. Point tests compare the solver directly at the experimental state. Curve tests compare trends such as CD(M), CP(M), CN(α), and Cm(α). Delta tests compare experimentally paired configurations such as body+fin minus body, fairings on minus off, jet on minus jet off, or fins on minus fins off. That third class is especially important because Suttles, L52D15a, TM 74058, and L54D27 all offer some form of paired-configuration information that can catch double-counting and missing interaction terms even when a total coefficient happens to look acceptable. 

The best first-pass error metrics are straightforward. Use relative error where the measured coefficient is comfortably nonzero, use range-normalized absolute error or slope error near zero crossings, and treat center-of-pressure error as a normalized axial-position error. Keep the 1% engineering target as a dashboard threshold, but store report-specific uncertainty separately rather than conflating “inside source uncertainty” with “within 1%.” That recommendation is not taken from any single source; it is a benchmark-design inference motivated by the fact that these reports mix free-flight CD data, whole-rocket CP curves, powered-plume coefficients, and dynamic derivatives with very different native accuracies and reference systems. 

Finally, the most important method-traceability rule is that every benchmark result should record which correlation was selected for each component and why. That is the only reliable way to validate a deterministic correlation stack rather than merely validating its final sum. For this dataset family, I would store at least: selected method, candidate methods rejected, declared validity envelope, whether the point was in-domain, and whether a fallback was used. The need for that is visible in the literature itself: RASAero’s ARCAS deck discusses Barrowman versus a modified method; L52D15a explicitly decomposes body, fin, and interference contributions; and L55G06a is largely a structured argument for understanding a complete configuration as the sum of isolated and interference effects.
